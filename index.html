Where this starts

When I was a kid, Puerto Rico defaulted on its debt. What followed was the largest municipal restructuring in US history, run by a federal oversight board created under a law called PROMESA — seven people, appointed rather than elected, with authority over the island's budget.

I didn't understand the mechanics at ten. I understood that something about how a place had been financed had broken, and that the consequences were going to shape the rest of my childhood: schools closing, people leaving, an argument about creditors that never quite ended.

Most people arrive at credit through a finance class. I arrived at it because I grew up inside a capital structure that stopped working, and I wanted to know why.

What I'm working out right now

The thing I keep circling is that the macro picture and the credit picture are currently telling you two different stories.

Rates have stayed higher for longer than most people expected, which is good news if you hold floating-rate paper — the coupons keep coming. But the same rates are squeezing the borrowers on the other side of those loans, and the pressure shows up in places the headline numbers don't capture.

~8%of BDC investment income now arriving as PIK
~40%of private credit borrowers with negative free cash flow
<2% vs ~5%headline default rate vs. the rate counting liability management

My working view is that the real risk isn't defaults. It's spread compression happening at the same time as credit deterioration — getting paid less for more risk, while capital keeps arriving anyway.

Which is why asset-based finance interests me more than direct lending at this point in the cycle. It's backed by collateral rather than by a borrower's cash flow, and it's less crowded.

I could be wrong about this. It's the kind of view I'd rather put in front of people who can tell me where it's naive than hold quietly and be pleased with.
